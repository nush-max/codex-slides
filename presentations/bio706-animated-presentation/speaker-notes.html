01 · Phage therapy

35 seconds

Simple explanation — study aid, not spoken

A bacteriophage is a virus that infects bacteria. Finding one that kills bacteria in a dish is only the beginning. We also need to make a clean, active product, get it to the infection, and show that it helps patients.

Graduate presentation script — spoken

Bacteriophage therapy asks whether viruses of bacteria can become useful treatments for difficult bacterial infections. My argument has three parts: match the biology, control the product, and prove the benefit. I will first explain how a phage kills a susceptible bacterium, then follow the biotechnology needed to turn that activity into a treatment. Two trials and one personalized case will show why those three requirements are connected but distinct.

Sources and optional technical detail — not spoken

Weiner et al. (2025), doi:10.1038/s41467-025-60598-4; Aslam et al. (2020), doi:10.1093/ofid/ofaa389. The illustration is an original simplified tailed-phage/bacterium schematic, not microscopy or a scale model.

02 · Antibiotic resistance calls for a different attack

55 seconds

Simple explanation — study aid, not spoken

Bacteria can resist an antibiotic by destroying it, changing its target, or pumping it out. A phage attacks through a different route. But an antibiotic-resistant bacterium is not automatically vulnerable to any phage: the particular phage still has to recognize and infect it.

Graduate presentation script — spoken

Antibiotic resistance creates a need for additional ways to attack bacteria. Mechanisms include enzymatic drug inactivation, alteration of a drug's target, and active efflux; the relevant mechanism depends on the drug and strain. A bacteriophage instead begins by recognizing a bacterial surface receptor and delivering its genome. If productive infection follows, the bacterium becomes the site of phage replication. That difference is the biological opportunity, but it is not a promise of universal susceptibility. Antibiotic resistance and susceptibility to a particular phage are distinct phenotypes. For example, the experimental phage OMKO1 interacts with an efflux-system surface component in Pseudomonas. We must therefore measure the phage–bacterium match rather than infer it from an antibiotic-resistance label.

Sources and optional technical detail — not spoken

Munita and Arias (2016), doi:10.1128/microbiolspec.VMBF-0016-2015; Chan et al. (2016), doi:10.1038/srep26717. OMKO1 uses OprM; this is a specific example, not a receptor shared by all phages. Reduced permeability and target protection are additional resistance mechanisms discussed in the review but not exhaustively listed on the slide.

03 · The lytic cycle turns one infection into progeny

75 seconds

Simple explanation — study aid, not spoken

Think of the bacterium as the place where the virus makes copies. The phage attaches, puts its genetic material inside, builds new phages, and releases them when the bacterium breaks open. This only happens when the host and conditions support infection. The drawing's few phages do not represent a measured number of offspring.

Graduate presentation script — spoken

The lytic cycle explains both the attraction of phage therapy and its dependence on bacterial biology. First, adsorption attaches a compatible phage to receptors on the bacterial surface. In this simplified tailed-phage model, the genome then enters the cell. Intracellular replication and synthesis of viral components precede assembly of progeny particles. Finally, host lysis releases new phages, which may infect other susceptible bacteria. The illustrated number of progeny is schematic, not a measured burst size. Contrast this productive lytic outcome with temperate phages, which can establish a prophage state instead of immediately killing the host. Therapeutic selection therefore favors reliably lytic behavior or suitable lytic derivatives. Importantly, self-amplification is conditional. A phage must encounter a susceptible host in a permissive environment; administering it does not guarantee that amplification will occur in a patient. This dependence leads directly to the matching problem.

Sources and optional technical detail — not spoken

Weiner et al. (2025), doi:10.1038/s41467-025-60598-4; Aslam et al. (2020), doi:10.1093/ofid/ofaa389. Temperate lifestyles are diverse: prophage integration is one possibility, not an exhaustive description of all phage states. Sarker et al. (2016), discussed in backup, provides a clinical example of limited intestinal amplification.

04 · Match the phage to the patient’s isolate

60 seconds

Simple explanation — study aid, not spoken

The same bacterial species can contain strains that respond differently to a phage. Researchers test the patient's actual bacterium. A clear patch in a bacterial lawn is a useful clue, but it can occur without the phage making offspring. Plaque-based tests help distinguish productive infection from simple clearing.

Graduate presentation script — spoken

The therapeutic unit is a particular phage interacting with a particular bacterial isolate. A conceptual workflow starts by identifying the infecting bacterium and testing candidate phages against that isolate. The key question is not simply whether a concentrated sample clears a patch of bacterial growth, but whether productive infection occurs. In a comparative experimental study, single-dilution spot tests often overestimated host range relative to efficiency-of-plating analyses. Clearing can occur without progeny production, including through lysis from without or abortive infection. Plaques and relative plating efficiency therefore provide complementary evidence about productive activity. These assays inform selection; they are not, by themselves, validated predictions of patient benefit. The figure is a conceptual explanation, not experimental plaque data or a proposed clinical susceptibility breakpoint.

Sources and optional technical detail — not spoken

Khan Mirzaei and Nilsson (2015), doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0118557; Weiner et al. (2025), doi:10.1038/s41467-025-60598-4. Efficiency of plating compares plaque formation on a test isolate with a reference host under the assay conditions. The study does not show that every appropriately interpreted spot assay is useless.

05 · Screen both the genome and the product

55 seconds

Simple explanation — study aid, not spoken

There are two different questions: is this the right virus, and is this a suitable product? Genome checks look for known undesirable genes. Product checks look for contaminating bacteria, bacterial toxins, and enough active phage. Passing one type of check does not replace the others.

Graduate presentation script — spoken

Matching establishes activity, but a therapeutic candidate must also pass biological and product-quality checks. In BX004-A development, genome screening supported a lytic lifestyle and screened for known toxin and antibiotic-resistance genes. Genomic identity and phenotype are complementary: sequence annotation does not eliminate every unknown function. Separately, product testing addresses sterility, endotoxin contamination, active titre, and stability. These are independent attributes. Removing viable production bacteria does not necessarily remove bacterial components such as endotoxin, and a sterile preparation may still have lost phage activity. The central biotechnology point is that an appropriate genome does not automatically make an appropriate medicine. Candidate characterization and final-product control must travel together through development and administration.

Sources and optional technical detail — not spoken

Weiner et al. (2025), doi:10.1038/s41467-025-60598-4; Aslam et al. (2020), doi:10.1093/ofid/ofaa389. These are reported development and clinical-series practices, not a claim that one uniform regulatory standard applies to every country and indication.

06 · Prepare a product that stays biologically active

65 seconds

Simple explanation — study aid, not spoken

Phages need bacteria to multiply, so their production starts with a suitable bacterial host. The final material must then be cleaned, put into a usable formulation, and tested. Counting genetic material is not the same as showing that the phages can still infect bacteria.

Graduate presentation script — spoken

Manufacturing translates the biological lifecycle into a product. At a conceptual level, phages are amplified using a suitable bacterial production host, followed by downstream separation and purification. Formulation then places the active particles in a vehicle compatible with the intended route. Release and stability testing assess identity, purity, and active concentration. Here, active titre concerns infective particles, commonly expressed as plaque-forming units, rather than simply the presence of viral material. BX004-A development also evaluated viability and delivery through a nebulizer. The lesson is that potency must survive the entire chain: manufacturing, storage, formulation, and administration. These are conceptual stages rather than a laboratory recipe. PhagoBurn will show why a product's activity at the point of treatment can be as important as the original phage selection.

Sources and optional technical detail — not spoken

Weiner et al. (2025), doi:10.1038/s41467-025-60598-4; Aslam et al. (2020), doi:10.1093/ofid/ofaa389; Jault et al. (2019), doi:10.1016/S1473-3099(18)30482-1. No operating parameters or procedural manufacturing recommendations are supplied.

07 · Delivery must bring active phages to the infection

60 seconds

Simple explanation — study aid, not spoken

The phage needs to get to the bacteria while still active. Putting it on a wound, inhaling it, and injecting it into a vein are different delivery problems. A dose that enters the body may not all reach the infected area, and laboratory susceptibility does not guarantee success.

Graduate presentation script — spoken

Route selection follows the infected compartment. PhagoBurn applied phages topically to accessible burn wounds. BX004-A used nebulization for chronic Pseudomonas infection in the lower respiratory tract. Selected personalized cases have used intravenous administration for disseminated infection, sometimes alongside topical treatment of accessible lesions. These routes are not interchangeable. The quantity administered is not the same as the quantity remaining active at the infection site. Delivery losses, clearance, neutralizing activity, and bacterial susceptibility can all affect exposure and response. Recovery of phages in sputum supports pulmonary delivery, but detection alone does not prove sustained replication. Likewise, activity against a cultured isolate does not guarantee success in the patient. We can now use two trials to ask which parts of this development chain were actually demonstrated.

Sources and optional technical detail — not spoken

Jault et al. (2019), doi:10.1016/S1473-3099(18)30482-1; Weiner et al. (2025), doi:10.1038/s41467-025-60598-4; Aslam et al. (2020), doi:10.1093/ofid/ofaa389; Dedrick et al. (2019), doi:10.1038/s41591-019-0437-z. The neutralizing-serum observation in the case series lacked a baseline comparison, so it cannot establish causation or population frequency.

08 · PhagoBurn exposed a product-performance gap

85 seconds

Simple explanation — study aid, not spoken

This trial did not show that the tested phage product beat standard treatment. Bacterial-load reduction took longer in the phage group. The product also lost much of its activity before patients received it. That is a major manufacturing problem, but it does not prove that a stronger dose would have worked.

Graduate presentation script — spoken

PhagoBurn was a randomized, double-blind phase one/two trial of a twelve-phage cocktail, PP1131, against Pseudomonas-infected burn wounds. Twenty-seven adults were randomized, and twenty-five entered the efficacy analysis: twelve phage recipients and thirteen standard-care recipients. The comparator was one-percent silver sulfadiazine. The primary endpoint was time to sustained reduction of bacterial burden using a swab-based scoring method, not clinical cure. Median time was one hundred forty-four hours with phages versus forty-seven hours with standard care. The hazard ratio of 0.29 therefore indicates slower attainment of the desired reduction with phages; its confidence interval was 0.10 to 0.79. A major complication was product potency: the intended concentration was ten to the sixth plaque-forming units per milliliter, but approximately ten to the second was delivered after titre declined. The study gives an unfavorable comparison at the delivered concentration and a clear manufacturing lesson. It does not prove that the intended concentration would have succeeded, nor that all phage approaches are ineffective.

Sources and optional technical detail — not spoken

Jault et al. (2019), doi:10.1016/S1473-3099(18)30482-1; PMID 30292481. Endpoint: sustained reduction by at least two quadrants on a four-quadrant swab method. P=0.018. Median confidence intervals: 144 h, 48–not reached; 47 h, 23–122. Efficacy and safety populations differ: do not substitute randomized denominators for analyzed denominators. Clinical details here are verified from the accessible abstract, not an assumed full-text review.

09 · BX004-A established delivery and an early signal

85 seconds

Simple explanation — study aid, not spoken

The inhaled product reached the lungs, and the small study found encouraging bacterial-count differences. But only nine people participated, with just two receiving placebo. It was mainly a safety study, so it cannot establish that patients breathe better or have fewer flare-ups.

Graduate presentation script — spoken

BX004-A tests a different part of the pipeline: a nebulized three-phage cocktail for adults with cystic fibrosis and chronic Pseudomonas infection. This published Part one study randomized seven patients to active treatment and two to placebo, with double blinding. Its primary safety and tolerability endpoints were met, and phages recovered from sputum supported delivery to the lower respiratory tract. The bacterial-density analyses were exploratory. Between-group differences favored treatment by 1.9 log-ten colony-forming units per gram on day four and 2.7 on day fifteen, with reported P values of 0.035 and 0.029. These are selected time-point contrasts, not a reconstructed continuous response curve. The two-person control group and exploratory endpoints sharply limit inference. The legitimate conclusion is feasible delivery and a microbiological signal that warrants further testing, rather than established improvements in lung function or exacerbations. A 2026 correction addressed preclinical transcriptomic sample inclusion; the authors stated that the results and conclusions were unaffected.

Sources and optional technical detail — not spoken

Weiner et al. (2025), doi:10.1038/s41467-025-60598-4; correction, Weiner et al. (2026), doi:10.1038/s41467-026-70146-3. Full-text clinical sections and correction reviewed. No treatment-related adverse events were reported in this nine-person study; this cannot exclude uncommon harms. Reductions were less pronounced on day eight. Do not interpret the two selected contrasts as the entire time course or as definitive multiplicity-adjusted clinical efficacy.

10 · Engineering made a personalized cocktail possible

60 seconds

Simple explanation — study aid, not spoken

For one patient, researchers found one naturally effective phage and modified or selected others to make a three-phage mixture. The patient improved during treatment, but this was not a controlled trial. The same mixture did not work well against other tested isolates, showing why personalization mattered.

Graduate presentation script — spoken

Dedrick and colleagues illustrate the biotechnology behind personalization. Their patient was a fifteen-year-old lung-transplant recipient with disseminated drug-resistant Mycobacterium abscessus. Muddy had natural activity against the isolate. ZoeJ was converted into a lytic derivative by deleting a repressor gene. For BPs, a lytic derivative and host-range selection were needed. The resulting three-phage cocktail was administered intravenously, with topical treatment of accessible lesions. Treatment was associated with sternal wound closure and improvement of skin nodules during combined clinical care. This is a compelling demonstration of engineering and treatment feasibility, but it is one uncontrolled case. The cocktail did not efficiently kill other tested clinical isolates, so the authors explicitly cautioned against generalizing it as a treatment for all such infections.

Sources and optional technical detail — not spoken

Dedrick et al. (2019), doi:10.1038/s41591-019-0437-z. Full text retrieved through official NCBI PMC. ZoeJΔ45 lacks repressor gene 45. BPsΔ33HTH-HRM10 includes a selected portal-gene 3 N270D substitution; do not call this tail-fiber redesign. Improved liver function was also reported. Association is not proof of cure, bacterial eradication, survival benefit or a phage-only causal effect.

11 · The studies test three different requirements

65 seconds

Simple explanation — study aid, not spoken

There are three separate checkpoints. A phage must infect the target bacterium, remain active and reach the infection, and then improve a meaningful patient outcome. A positive result at one checkpoint is useful, but it cannot substitute for evidence at the next one.

Graduate presentation script — spoken

The evidence becomes more coherent when we organize it around three requirements. First is activity against the relevant isolate: the personalized case shows what matching and engineering can make possible. Second is active exposure at the infected site: PhagoBurn illustrates loss of titre, while BX004-A demonstrates pulmonary delivery. Third is a patient benefit attributable to treatment. That requires appropriate controls, sufficient sample size, and clinically meaningful endpoints, alongside microbiological measures. This three-part framework is my synthesis of the studies, not a scoring system validated by their authors. It explains why a successful plaque assay, phage recovery in sputum, and improvement during compassionate use are all informative but answer different questions. A convincing development program must connect those answers rather than treat them as interchangeable proof of efficacy.

Sources and optional technical detail — not spoken

Presentation synthesis from Dedrick et al. (2019), Jault et al. (2019), Weiner et al. (2025), and Aslam et al. (2020). The proposed trial priorities are recommendations based on these limitations, not claims that one universal trial design suits every indication.

12 · Build the treatment—and test the benefit

55 seconds

Simple explanation — study aid, not spoken

The main takeaway is not simply that phages are promising. It is that their promise depends on the entire development chain. For discussion, consider whether killing more bacteria is enough if the patient feels no better or the infection returns.

Graduate presentation script — spoken

Phage therapy is best understood as a biotechnology pipeline rather than a single antibacterial trick. Its biological specificity creates an opportunity, but also a matching requirement. Manufacturing and delivery must preserve activity, and clinical studies must show that this activity matters to patients. So the argument returns to three phrases: match the biology, control the product, and prove the benefit. The studies support continued development for defined indications, while cautioning against a broad claim that phages replace antibiotics. To open discussion: if bacterial burden falls but the patient does not improve, what has the treatment demonstrated, and what endpoint would you require in the next trial? The remaining slides provide the full references and optional evidence for discussion.

16 · How the wider evidence fits

outside timed talk

Simple explanation — study aid, not spoken

This slide is only for questions. Small encouraging studies, unsuccessful trials, and rescue cases all teach something, but they do not combine into one reliable percentage of success. Phage resistance can sometimes carry an antibiotic-sensitivity tradeoff, but that is not guaranteed.

Graduate presentation script — optional, outside timed talk

The broader literature reinforces the same evidence distinctions. Wright's twenty-four-person otitis trial reported preliminary improvement in a selected susceptible population. Sarker's interim cohort of one hundred twenty children showed no detected diarrhea benefit and limited gut amplification; it was not restricted to confirmed multidrug-resistant infections. Aslam reported seven author-defined successes among ten heterogeneous intravenous cases, with concurrent antibiotics and overlap with an earlier case report. TP-102 randomized nineteen participants and was underpowered to demonstrate healing superiority. Separately, Chan's laboratory work showed that OMKO1 resistance could alter an efflux-related antibiotic-sensitivity tradeoff. That is a preclinical evolutionary mechanism, not a universal clinical resensitization effect. These findings should be interpreted within their designs and indications rather than converted into a single success rate.

Sources and optional technical detail — not spoken

Wright et al. (2009); Sarker et al. (2016); Aslam et al. (2020); Nir-Paz et al. (2025); Chan et al. (2016). Full references are on slides 14–15. TP-102's infected-subgroup 50% closure counts were 5/7 versus 1/3, P=0.500; these do not show superiority. Aslam's series includes the previously reported Acinetobacter case, so the two reports must not be counted as independent patients.
